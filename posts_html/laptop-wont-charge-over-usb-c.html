Why Your Laptop Won't Charge Over USB-C

Plain English • No sponsored placements
Written and checked by Simple Product Picks · last updated October 2026
✓ Plain-English explainer ✓ No sponsored placements

The short answerA laptop that will not charge over USB-C can fail at any of four points, so check them in order, starting with the one that's easy to miss: not every USB-C port charges. Dell's support page notes that a port marked with a "D" logo carries power delivery while one marked with a SuperSpeed (SS) logo "only supports data transferring", so a charger in the wrong socket does nothing. If the port is right, the charger has to be strong enough (for its Macs, Apple advises an adapter that "supports USB Power Delivery" and gives at least the wattage of the adapter recommended for that Mac), and PCWorld notes an underpowered one can leave a laptop that normally wants 65W unable to charge "while it's being used". Then comes the cable, which carries its own rating (Anker builds its USB-C cables in 60W, 100W, 140W and 240W versions), and only after those do the ordinary debris-and-reseat checks apply.

Start with the port, not the charger

A USB-C socket can carry data, video or power in any combination, and the laptop's maker decides which. So before you swap any gear, look at the small icon printed beside each USB-C port. On a Dell, you want the "D" rather than the SS; on other laptops, the spec page names the port that charges. The tell is a laptop that charges from one socket and ignores an identical one next to it. If that's you, the fix costs nothing: use the right port.

Is the charger strong enough?

Next, the charger has to speak USB Power Delivery and reach your laptop's wattage. Apple puts it this way for its own machines: "If your Mac uses USB-C to charge, you can charge your Mac laptop with any USB-C power adapter or display that supports USB Power Delivery (USB PD)." The same check works for any USB-C laptop. Find the wattage printed on the laptop's own charger, or on its spec page, and use a charger that matches or beats it. That's why a phone charger can fall short: PCWorld's example is a 15- or 20-watt phone charger on a laptop that normally runs on a 65-watt one.

The cable has a rating too

The right charger can still be let down by the lead, because a USB-C cable only passes the power it's rated to carry. Use the cable from the laptop's own charger if it had one, or a spare rated for at least the wattage you need. Our fast-charging cable guide covers what the ratings mean and which leads carry full laptop power.

When it is the port, the cable, or the laptop itself

If the port, the charger and the cable all check out and it still won't charge, change one thing at a time. First try a charger you know works on another device, then a cable you know works, in the port that should charge. Whichever swap makes it charge points to the faulty part. Before you blame the hardware, PCWorld's advice is to "look at the plug as well as the receptacles on your device for foreign debris or damage under a good light and perhaps with a magnifying glass". Clean what you find with the laptop switched off, then reseat the plug firmly. If a known-good charger and cable still do nothing in a clean charging port, the fault is likely the port or the laptop's charging circuit. That's the point to seek a repair.

FAQ

Q: Why does my laptop charge when it's off but not when I'm using it? A: That points to an underpowered charger. A laptop draws more while you work than while it sleeps. So a charger below its rating can top up the battery overnight and still lose ground during the day. Match the charger to the wattage on your laptop's own charger and the pattern should stop.
