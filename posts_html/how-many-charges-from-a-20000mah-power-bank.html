How Many Charges Do You Get From A 20000mAh Power Bank?

The short answer: fewer than the number on the box

A 20000mAh power bank does not put 20000mAh into your phone. The figure on the front is the capacity of the cells inside, measured at their own low voltage, and a real part of it is spent getting the energy out of the bank and up to the voltage your phone charges at. Plan around a smaller number.

For a quick count of full charges, divide the bank's usable capacity by your phone's battery size in mAh. A phone that holds around 4000mAh gets roughly three full top-ups from a typical 20000mAh bank, four at best, not the five the headline hints at, and charging fast or using a long cable trims it a little further.

Why the headline figure never reaches your phone

The cells in a power bank store their energy at a low voltage. UGREEN puts it plainly: "The internal lithium cells usually sit at 3.7V, but your phone needs power delivered at 5V, 9V, or higher via USB," and "Stepping up this voltage consumes energy, which is why your gear gets warm, that's literally your battery percentage turning into wasted heat."

So the mAh on the box is counted at the cells' 3.7V, while your phone runs on a battery at a different voltage again, and the two only compare once you put them on the same footing. Anker gives a worked figure: a cell rated "10,000mAh at 3.6V" works out to "approximately 9,300mAh" once it is expressed at the 3.87V a phone battery runs at. Two ordinary losses come on top, both of which Anker quantifies for its own banks: "Not all energy converts during charging; typically, 10-15% is lost," and "The cable used for charging also incurs some energy loss; the longer the cable, the greater the loss, typically around 5%." None of this is a fault; it is how every power bank works.

Working out your own number

The figure worth using is the rated capacity, which Anker defines as "the battery's capacity at the rated voltage": the amount the bank actually hands over, not the capacity of the cells inside. Many makers print it in small type near the model number or the safety marks; if yours shows only the big number, treat the usable amount as clearly less than the label.

Then the sum is simple. Divide the rated capacity by your phone's battery in mAh, which the phone maker lists on its own spec page. Say the usable figure is around 12000mAh and your phone battery is 4000mAh: that is three charges on paper, give or take, and the exact figure shifts with your phone's battery size and how efficiently the bank runs. Small accessories go the other way, because an earbuds case or a watch holds only a few hundred mAh, so the same bank refills them many times over.

FAQ

Q: How many times will a 20000mAh power bank charge my phone? A: Work it from the usable capacity, not the 20000mAh headline. Divide the rated figure by your phone's battery size in mAh; a phone around 4000mAh usually gets about three full charges from a 20000mAh bank, four at best, and fewer if you charge fast or use a long cable.

Q: Why don't I get the full 20000mAh? A: The cells store energy at about 3.7V and your phone is fed at 5V or more, so stepping the voltage up loses some as heat. Anker also puts conversion loss at "10-15%" and cable loss at "around 5%" for its own banks.

Q: What is rated capacity? A: Anker defines it as "the battery's capacity at the rated voltage", meaning the energy the bank actually delivers rather than the capacity of the cells inside. It is the honest number for working out charges, and makers usually print it in small type on the bank.

Q: Does a bigger mAh number always mean more charges? A: Broadly yes, but only comparing like with like. Two banks both labelled 20000mAh can deliver different usable amounts depending on their efficiency, so the rated capacity, not the headline mAh, is what decides how many charges you really get.
